What to include in a contract for commercial cleaning services in Washington

A complete guide to commercial cleaning contract clauses, pricing models, insurance requirements and service agreements for Pierce, King and Snohomish counties.

By Mike Bennett, Content Writer · November 23, 2026

A commercial cleaning contract should spell out the scope of work (which areas, what cleaning methods, restocking duties), pricing model (flat monthly fee, per square foot, or hourly), facility-specific requirements (such as OSHA sanitation for medical and dental practices), contract term with a termination-for-convenience clause, insurance and bonding requirements, service-level agreements with quality-inspection procedures, and escalation clauses tied to Washington's annual minimum-wage adjustments. Without these elements in writing, you risk scope creep, hidden charges, service inconsistency, and disputes over who pays when something goes wrong.

Introduction

A commercial cleaning contract is a binding agreement between a facility owner, manager, or tenant and a cleaning vendor that defines what work will be done, how often, at what price, and under what terms. If you manage an office, medical practice, retail space, school, warehouse, or multi-tenant property in Pierce, King, or Snohomish County, Washington, you've probably shopped for cleaning services or renewed a contract. The difference between a vague handshake deal and a written contract is the difference between guessing what you're paying for and knowing exactly what you get.

This guide covers the clauses and terms that protect both you and your vendor, with a focus on Washington state requirements. We'll walk through scope of work, pricing models, contract terms, insurance, and quality standards. We'll also address facility-specific needs, such as medical and dental sanitation, and what property managers should know when managing multiple buildings or coordinating with tenant cleaning vendors.

Why a written contract matters: a good one removes ambiguity. Without it, one party thinks "weekly cleaning" means restrooms and common areas; the other thinks it's restrooms, common areas, conference rooms and windows. One thinks the price is flat; the other thinks it's per square foot. One thinks the vendor covers floor care; the other thinks the client does. These gaps cost time, money and frustration. A written scope, pricing model, term and service-level agreement prevent that waste.

Washington state law also sets the floor for labor costs in any cleaning contract. Washington's minimum wage for 2026 is $17.13 per hour, and the state adjusts it each year using the Consumer Price Index for Wage Earners. Several cities in the Puget Sound region, including Seattle, Renton, Everett and unincorporated King County, set local minimum wages higher than the state floor. Any multi-year contract without a wage escalation clause will squeeze the vendor's labor budget and can lead to turnover, missed visits or staff shortcuts. We'll cover why that matters.

The commercial cleaning market in the U.S. is projected to reach $84.00 billion in 2026, and most of that value flows through written contracts. Getting yours right sets the tone for a stable, transparent relationship that lasts.

Scope of work, frequency and facility type define what you're actually paying for

A written scope of work lists exactly which areas get cleaned, what methods apply to each surface, and what supplies the vendor provides or you furnish. This prevents scope creep (the vendor cleaning more than agreed and charging for it later) and lets you compare bids fairly.

Your scope should identify every room or zone: lobby, hallway, restrooms, kitchen, conference rooms, offices, stairwells, and common areas. For each, note the specific tasks: vacuuming vs. hot-water extraction for carpet; dusting and wiping vs. stripping and waxing for hard floors; glass cleaning with frames and sills vs. a quick squeegee pass. Restocking tasks matter too: paper towels, soap, trash liners, and how often they're refilled. If a vendor claims they don't know what "clean" means without a written scope, that's a warning.

Cleaning frequency depends on your facility type and traffic:

  • Offices and retail: typically daily or Monday-Wednesday-Friday; high-traffic areas may need daily restroom checks and disinfection
  • Medical and dental practices: OSHA-aware sanitation with bloodborne-pathogen training for staff; infection-control color-coded equipment; daily restroom sanitation
  • Schools and childcare: sanitizing protocols for high-touch surfaces (doorknobs, light switches, desks); age-specific disinfection standards; compliance with state childcare licensing rules
  • Warehouses and industrial: floor care suited to concrete, epoxy, or polished finishes; dock and loading-area cleanup; dust control

Facility-specific add-ons should be listed in the scope, not discovered as surprise charges later: exterior windows and pressure washing, floor stripping and waxing, post-construction cleanup, emergency response after water damage or spills. If your building has an atrium, skylights, or high exterior walls, ask in writing whether the vendor can handle them and at what cost.

Without a detailed scope, it is harder to hold a vendor accountable when cleaning misses expectations or to compare competing bids fairly.

Pricing models in commercial cleaning contracts and how labor costs drive them

Commercial cleaning contracts use three main pricing models: per-square-foot (a fixed rate applied to the cleanable area, which varies by facility type), hourly (time and materials, most common for one-time work or emergency calls), and flat monthly fee (the most common for recurring janitorial service, based on a walkthrough scope and set for the contract term).

Flat monthly fees protect you from surprise charges and make budgeting predictable. The vendor quotes a price after visiting your facility, estimating labor hours and materials. That price stays stable unless you change the scope in writing.

Labor represents 65 to 75 percent of contract value, so wage increases directly raise your bill. Washington's minimum wage is $17.13 per hour as of 2026 and adjusts annually with the Consumer Price Index. Several cities in your region set higher local minimums: Seattle, SeaTac, Tukwila, Renton, Everett, and unincorporated King County all exceed the state floor. A multi-year contract without a wage-escalation clause exposes a vendor to margin pressure and staffing turnover, which degrades service consistency. If your vendor skimps on labor to stay profitable, you get a skeleton crew or high turnover instead of the same trained team visit after visit.

Look for a price-escalation clause tied to the annual CPI-W adjustment or local wage increases, not an open-ended hike. This keeps labor costs transparent and vendors incentivized to retain trained staff. For example, a contract might read: "Annual pricing adjustment capped at the January Consumer Price Index-Wage Earners increase or the local minimum-wage increase in the jurisdiction where the facility is located, whichever is higher."

Avoid contracts that quote a price without a walkthrough; accurate pricing depends on facility size, layout, soil level, and special finishes. Rainier starts every recurring account with a free walkthrough before quoting a flat monthly price.

Essential contract for commercial cleaning services clauses: Term, renewal, termination and insurance

Contract term should suit your flexibility needs. A one-year initial term with a 30-day termination-for-convenience clause (either side can exit with 30 days' notice) balances commitment with exit flexibility. Auto-renewal clauses are common but should require an active renewal decision, not a silent rollover into year two. Many disputes arise when one party forgets to give notice and the contract auto-renews.

No long-term lock-in: a good contract lets you terminate without penalty if service quality drops or your needs change. Rainier Facility Solutions uses a simple service agreement either side can end with 30 days' notice, so you are not locked into a long-term commitment.

Insurance and bonding are non-negotiable:

  • General liability: set a minimum limit with your insurance advisor; covers damage to your building or third-party injury caused by the cleaning crew
  • Workers' compensation: covers employee injuries; ask for proof of coverage before work begins
  • Surety bonding: covers theft or damage by individual team members; request a certificate for each crew member

Request a certificate of insurance naming your company as an additional insured before the first visit. Don't start work without proof.

Indemnification and liability clauses protect both parties. The vendor assumes liability for damage caused by their crew and agrees to defend you if an employee injures a third party or damages property. For example: "Vendor shall indemnify, defend, and hold harmless Client from any claims, damages, or liability arising from Vendor's negligence, breach of contract, or employee misconduct." Confirm the vendor's insurance is active and in force, not just promised.

Service level agreements, quality inspections and what to do when cleaning misses the standard

A service level agreement (SLA) sets measurable performance standards so both you and your vendor know what "clean" means. It specifies which restrooms are stocked and sanitized, defines "no visible dust" on surfaces, requires floors to be swept and mopped or vacuumed on schedule, confirms trash and recycling are removed, and documents disinfection of high-touch surfaces like door handles and light switches. Tying these benchmarks to written, measurable standards helps keep disputes over quality from coming down to opinion.

Quality inspections are your enforcement tool. The contract should allow supervisor walkthroughs by the vendor's own team and spot-checks by yours, with a written report documented within 24 hours of any misses. Rainier's crews re-clean affected areas at no charge if you report an issue in writing within 24 hours. This recourse is what separates a cleaning contract with teeth from one that leaves you helpless when quality drops.

Red flags in cleaning contracts include no SLA at all, no right to inspect or report issues, no recourse if the standard slips, or a re-cleaning fee instead of free performance correction. These clauses leave you with leverage only on paper. For medical and dental practices, the SLA must specify OSHA-aware sanitation, color-coded equipment to prevent cross-contamination, and bloodborne-pathogen training for any crew handling biohazard cleanup. Schools and childcare facilities need documented sanitizing of high-touch surfaces and confirmation of products safe for children. Without these specifics in writing, you have no way to verify compliance or hold the vendor accountable.

Multi-tenant, HOA and property management considerations for cleaning contracts

Multi-tenant buildings and HOAs face contract complexity that single-occupancy offices don't: vendors must respect tenant access rules, security protocols, badge requirements, alarm codes and parking restrictions. The contract should specify which common areas are cleaned (lobbies, hallways, restrooms, loading docks, parking lots), cleaning frequency for each zone, and whether the vendor coordinates with other tenants or reports directly to the property manager. Scope disputes stem from unclear responsibility. Is the vendor cleaning tenant-occupied suites or common areas only? Who replaces supplies like paper towels and soap? Can tenants request extra cleanings at their cost? Written scope prevents these conflicts and protects both the property manager and the vendor.

Bid comparison for property managers should include the vendor's insurance certificate, background checks on crew members, and references from similar buildings. Request the vendor's response time to service issues and emergency calls (spills, sewage backups, flooding). A 24/7 emergency line is worth asking about. Board approval and vendor insurance are linked: the building's property insurance and liability coverage depend on vendors carrying adequate insurance and naming the building as an additional insured. Never hire a cleaning company without proof of active general liability and workers' compensation coverage.

When evaluating proposals, compare scope depth across vendors using this checklist:

  • Does the proposal list each common area separately with cleaning frequency?
  • Does it specify restroom supplies (paper, soap, hand sanitizer) included or billed separately?
  • Is the vendor's response time to emergency calls documented (same day, next business day, 24/7)?
  • Are background checks and drug screening confirmed in writing?
  • Does the insurance certificate name your building as additional insured?
  • Is there a supervisor walkthrough schedule and inspection protocol?
  • What is the notice period to end the contract, and are there auto-renewal clauses?

A vendor who can answer each of these without hesitation and in writing is more likely to deliver consistent service. For multi-site portfolios across several buildings, one schedule, one point of contact and one invoice cycle reduce administrative overhead. Request references from property managers running similar multi-building programs.

Conclusion

A well-written commercial cleaning contract for services protects both parties by defining scope, pricing, insurance, quality standards and termination rights. Without a scope of work tied to facility type, pricing model and frequency, you're guessing at what you'll actually receive. Without an SLA and inspection rights, quality will slip over time and you'll have no recourse. For multi-tenant and HOA properties, vendor insurance, security protocols and board approval are non-negotiable before cleaning begins.

The contract is not just a legal safeguard. It's a roadmap that lets you hire a vendor you can trust, know what to expect each visit, and hold them to it. Most facility managers and property managers in Pierce, King and Snohomish counties choose a cleaning partner who shows up consistently, communicates when issues arise, and treats the building as if it matters.

Key takeaways:

  • An SLA with measurable standards (restrooms stocked, no visible dust, high-touch surfaces disinfected) and inspection rights gives you leverage to enforce quality.
  • Require a written scope naming each area, frequency, supplies included, and pricing model; a re-cleaning clause at no charge for issues reported within 24 hours.
  • For medical, dental, school and childcare facilities, specify OSHA-aware sanitation, bloodborne-pathogen training, color-coded equipment and documented compliance in the contract.
  • Multi-tenant and HOA contracts must address badge access, alarm codes, tenant rules and which common areas are cleaned; get a vendor insurance certificate naming your building as additional insured before work begins.
  • A vendor with 24/7 emergency response, supervisor walkthroughs and a direct account manager is more likely to keep the standard consistent from visit one to visit 50.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a cleaning contract and a service agreement?

A cleaning contract and a service agreement are functionally the same document: a binding agreement between you and a cleaning vendor that spells out scope, price, term and performance standards. Some vendors call it a "contract," others a "service agreement." The name doesn't matter; what matters is that it's written, signed by both parties, and includes scope of work, pricing, term, insurance, termination rights and what happens if quality misses the mark.

How do I know if a cleaning vendor is properly insured and bonded in Washington?

Ask the vendor for a certificate of insurance showing general liability, workers' compensation, and surety bonding, and confirm the coverage limits with your insurance advisor. The certificate should name your building or company as additional insured. Call the vendor's insurance broker to verify the policy is active, and request an updated certificate annually before renewal. Don't sign a contract until you have proof in writing.

Can a commercial cleaning contract be terminated early if I'm not satisfied with the work?

Yes, if the contract includes a termination-for-convenience clause (also called termination at will). Most professional vendors offer a 30-day termination notice provision, so either side can exit with 30 days' written notice. Without this clause, you may be locked in for the full term. Before signing, confirm the notice period and whether there are any early-termination fees.

What should I ask for before signing a contract for commercial cleaning services?

Request a written scope of work (listing each area, cleaning method and frequency), a detailed price breakdown (labor, supplies, equipment), proof of insurance and bonding, an SLA with performance standards and inspection procedures, the cleaning vendor's background-check and drug-screening policy, the supervisor's contact information and walkthrough schedule, and confirmation of the termination notice period and any auto-renewal terms.

How often should a commercial cleaning contract be reviewed or renegotiated?

Review your contract annually, especially before renewal. Washington's minimum wage adjusts each year by the CPI-W; on Jan. 1, 2026, it rose 2.8% to $17.13 per hour. If your vendor has a wage-pass-through clause, your price will reflect this. Local minimums in Seattle, SeaTac, Tukwila, Renton, Everett and unincorporated King County are higher. Renegotiate if scope has changed, labor costs have risen more than your price increase, or quality has slipped.

What happens if a cleaning vendor damages my property or causes an injury on my site?

The vendor's general liability insurance covers property damage caused by the crew, and their workers' compensation covers employee injuries. The contract should include an indemnification clause stating the vendor will defend and pay you for claims arising from their negligence or breach. If damage occurs, report it in writing within the timeframe the contract specifies (commonly five business days), and file a claim with the vendor's insurance broker. Keep photos and documentation.

Are there Washington-specific labor or compliance rules I should include in a cleaning contract?

Yes. Washington requires all employers to carry workers' compensation insurance (verify via L&I). Washington has paid sick leave requirements for employees, so ask the vendor to confirm in writing that it complies. If your facility is a medical or dental practice, the vendor's crews must be trained in OSHA bloodborne-pathogen standards and hazard communication. Schools and childcare facilities should specify their sanitizing and disinfection requirements in the scope of work. Include these requirements in the scope of work or as an attachment, and ask the vendor for proof of training before the contract begins.
