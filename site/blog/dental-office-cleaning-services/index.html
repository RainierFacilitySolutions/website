Introduction

If you run a dental practice in Pierce, King or Snohomish County, you know that cleaning goes beyond aesthetics. Patients notice a spotless waiting room, but regulators notice whether your practice follows Washington's decontamination requirements and whether your cleaning contractor is trained in bloodborne pathogen safety. This guide covers what dental office cleaning services actually include, what Washington law requires, why crew training matters for your liability, and how to choose a contractor that understands dental environments instead of treating your practice like a generic office.

Dental offices face unique cleaning demands. The CDC expects staff who perform environmental infection prevention to receive job-specific training at hire, when procedures change, and at least annually. A practice that hires a janitorial contractor without defining which surfaces the contractor cleans and which stay with clinical staff can end up with gaps in its documentation. We'll walk through the scope of work, Washington's written cleaning schedule requirement, what training a contractor's crews should have, what to expect during routine visits, and the verification steps before you sign an agreement. By the end, you'll know whether your current contractor or a new one meets your practice's standards.

What dental office cleaning services actually cover (and what they don't)

Dental office cleaning services provided by a janitorial contractor cover housekeeping surfaces: floors, restrooms, waiting rooms, reception areas, hallways, trash and recycling, door handles and light switches, sinks in common areas, and exterior windows or sidewalks. The contractor does not enter operatories with instruments, sharps containers, or clinical equipment, and does not handle regulated medical waste. That scope stays with your clinical staff.

The CDC separates clinical contact surfaces (dental chairs, spit trays, light handles) which your staff disinfects between patients using EPA-registered hospital disinfectants, from housekeeping surfaces like floors and walls which a contractor can clean with soap and water. If a housekeeping surface is visibly contaminated with blood, it must be disinfected; routine surfaces are cleaned with soap and water.

The boundary matters because it defines liability. Your contractor is responsible for the restroom, the waiting room floor, and the hallway. Your staff is responsible for the operatory and what happens inside it. A contractor who doesn't understand this boundary may enter operatories to clean, mixing their housekeeping tools with clinical areas, or leave visible contamination because they assumed clinical staff handle it.

A written scope of work prevents this. Before a contractor starts, your practice should document which areas they clean, how often, what products they use, and when they visit. If you're in a multi-operatory practice or a shared building with badge access, the scope should also specify how the contractor gains entry and whether they work after hours. Many dental practices in Pierce, King and Snohomish Counties schedule janitorial service after the last patient or on weekends, so cleaning doesn't interrupt patient care. A contractor experienced in dental offices knows this rhythm and plans around alarm codes and key access instead of showing up mid-morning.

Washington State cleaning and decontamination requirements for dental offices

Washington's bloodborne pathogen rule, WAC 296-823-14055, requires employers to develop a written cleaning and decontamination schedule based on location in the facility, surface type, type of contamination, and the tasks performed. This schedule must be documented and updated when procedures change. It is not optional, and it is not a list you create once and file away.

Work surfaces must be decontaminated after a procedure, immediately (or as soon as feasible) after a spill or visible contamination, and at the end of the workshift if the surface could have been contaminated since the last cleaning. Your practice develops and follows this schedule for clinical surfaces. For housekeeping surfaces your contractor handles, the schedule should specify how often the restroom is disinfected, whether the floor is wet-mopped or vacuumed first, and what disinfectant is used if blood contamination occurs.

Washington L&I also applies WAC 296-901, the chemical hazard communication standard, to janitorial work. Every cleaner, disinfectant, air freshener and other product must have a Safety Data Sheet (SDS) on file, and the product must be labeled correctly. Your contractor's crews must receive training on each product they use, including when to use it, how long to let it sit on a surface (called contact or dwell time), and what PPE to wear.

A contractor that is bonded, insured and trained in Washington's bloodborne pathogen rules can help your practice maintain this documentation. They should provide a copy of their written cleaning schedule tied to your practice's layout, confirm that their products have SDS documentation, and confirm that their crews have received hazard communication training. This isn't red tape. It's proof that if an inspection or incident occurs, your practice followed the rules.

Why crew training and bloodborne pathogen certification matters for your liability

The CDC Infection Prevention Checklist for Dental Settings expects that staff who perform environmental infection prevention get job-specific training at hire, when procedures or policies change, and at least annually. A cleaning contractor's crews are part of your practice's infection prevention team when they work in your facility. If they lack training in bloodborne pathogen exposure, cleaning vs. disinfection, or what to do if they contact blood, they create a liability for your practice.

Bloodborne pathogen training covers routes of exposure (needlestick, mucous membrane contact, non-intact skin), how to recognize areas that may be contaminated, the difference between cleaning (removing organic material) and disinfection (killing pathogens), use of appropriate PPE, and incident reporting. Untrained cleaners may touch a surface without gloves, may clean an area without disinfecting, or may handle a sharps container. Each is an exposure event that can lead to infection or a workers' compensation claim against your practice.

A contractor whose crews are bonded, background-checked, drug-screened and trained in bloodborne pathogen rules protects your practice from liability if contamination is missed or a crew member is exposed. Equally important is documentation. A contractor that maintains logs of training dates, cleaning visits, products used, and areas cleaned creates an audit trail that your dental board, your insurance carrier, and L&I will expect to see. This isn't paperwork. It's evidence that your practice exercised due diligence in selecting and monitoring a contractor.

When evaluating a contractor, ask for proof of training completion (a certificate or log), not just a promise. Ask what their procedure is if they discover blood or sharp objects. Ask how they handle keys, alarm codes and access to your operatories. A contractor that answers these questions clearly and provides documentation is signaling that they understand the stakes.

Dental office cleaning checklist: What to expect at each type of visit

Daily or Monday-Wednesday-Friday janitorial visits handle the routine work that keeps your practice running without disruption to patient care. These visits happen after-hours: waiting room and hallway vacuuming, restroom sanitation (toilets, sinks, mirrors, floors, trash refill), surface wiping of door handles and high-touch areas, and trash removal. The timing matters because it means your staff arrives to a clean practice every morning.

Restroom sanitation is where compliance gets specific. Per the CDC, housekeeping surfaces such as sinks can be cleaned with soap and water, and should be disinfected if visibly contaminated with blood. The CDC says housekeeping surfaces can be cleaned with soap and water and need disinfection when visibly contaminated with blood, so ask your contractor which products they use in restrooms and when. A contractor familiar with dental offices knows which disinfectants work and how long they need to sit (contact time) to kill pathogens.

Weekly or monthly deep cleans add floors (vacuuming and mopping), window and glass cleaning, restroom grout and tile work, and disinfection of high-touch areas like light switches, door frames and railings. This routine keeps waiting rooms and common areas looking professional and reduces the spread of illness during cold and flu season. Many practices schedule these on Fridays or after-hours on weekdays to avoid patient-facing disruption.

A written scope ensures your practice and the contractor agree on frequency, areas covered, and products used. Changes to the schedule (such as adding weekend service or shifting to twice-weekly restroom cleaning) are quoted in writing before they take effect. This prevents surprise invoices and keeps expectations clear on both sides.

Conclusion

Hiring a dental office cleaning contractor is not the same as hiring an office cleaner. The stakes include patient safety, staff protection, compliance with Washington State rules, and your practice's reputation if infection control fails. The difference between a contractor that knows the rules and one that does not often comes down to training, documentation, and consistency. A contractor that provides proof of crew training, maintains audit logs, disinfects with EPA-registered products, and assigns the same team every visit is signaling that they understand bloodborne pathogens, chemical hazards and the written schedules your practice needs to stay compliant.

The most common mistake is choosing based on price alone and discovering too late that the contractor doesn't know the difference between a clinical contact surface and a housekeeping surface, or that their crews aren't trained in bloodborne pathogen safety. The least expensive option often costs the most in the end.

Key takeaways:

  • EPA-registered hospital disinfectants are needed for clinical contact surfaces and for any housekeeping surface visibly contaminated with blood.
  • Look for crews that are W-2 employees, bonded, insured, background-checked and drug-screened, with documented annual training in bloodborne pathogen exposure and hazard communication.
  • Request a written scope of work, flat monthly price, and proof of training completion before you hire.
  • Ask how the contractor handles keys, alarm codes, operatory access and discovered sharps.
  • Choose a local contractor familiar with after-hours service, dental-specific timing and Washington compliance rules.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between cleaning and disinfection in a dental office?

Cleaning removes dirt and organic matter with soap and water; disinfection uses an EPA-registered hospital disinfectant to kill pathogens. Clinical contact surfaces in dental settings must be both cleaned and disinfected with an EPA-registered hospital disinfectant after each patient. Housekeeping surfaces like floors and walls can be cleaned with soap and water unless visibly contaminated with blood, in which case disinfection is required.

Can a dental office use standard commercial cleaning products, or do they need EPA-registered hospital disinfectants?

Clinical contact surfaces need an EPA-registered hospital disinfectant after each patient, while routine housekeeping surfaces can be cleaned with soap and water and disinfected if visibly contaminated with blood. Ask your contractor which products they bring and who supplies them.

How often should a dental office deep clean floors and restrooms?

Routine janitorial visits (daily, Monday-Wednesday-Friday, or weekly) handle daily restroom sanitation and floor vacuuming. Many practices add periodic deep cleans of floors and restrooms to remove embedded soil and buildup that routine visits don't reach; your contractor can recommend a frequency based on patient volume and traffic. Your contractor can recommend a frequency based on patient volume and traffic.

What should I look for in a dental office cleaning contract to avoid hidden fees?

Ask for a written, room-by-room scope of work and a flat monthly price before you sign. The scope should list which areas are cleaned, how often, and what products are used. Verify that the quote includes restroom supplies (paper towels, soap, toilet paper) and that any add-ons (such as window cleaning or extra floor care) are quoted separately in writing before work begins.

If our cleaning contractor finds blood or body fluid contamination, what happens next?

The contractor should report the discovery immediately to your practice manager or clinical lead and document it in writing. Under Washington's bloodborne pathogen rule (WAC 296-823-14055), work surfaces must be decontaminated with an appropriate disinfectant as soon as possible after a spill or visible contamination. The contractor should clean and disinfect the area; your clinical staff investigates the source and takes follow-up measures.

How does a dental office document cleaning compliance for insurance and board audits?

Your contractor should maintain a cleaning log that records the date, time, areas cleaned, crew names and any issues found. Keep copies of your written cleaning schedule (tied to WAC 296-823), proof that crews are trained in bloodborne pathogens and hazard communication, and a copy of the contractor's certificates of insurance. Store these documents with your infection control records so you can produce them if an auditor or insurance company asks.
